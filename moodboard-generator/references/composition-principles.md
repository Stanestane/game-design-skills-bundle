# Moodboard Composition & Color Reference

Read this before curating images or laying out a board. It's the difference between
"a grid of pretty pictures" and a board that actually reads as one coherent idea.

## 1. A hero is optional, and never the whole width

If the theme has one clearly standout image, give it more visual weight than the
rest — but "more weight" means roughly 2-4x a secondary tile's area, not the entire
top of the board. A hero that spans the full board width crowds out everything else
and stops reading as "part of a composition" — it just becomes a banner with a photo
grid underneath it. A hero should still sit alongside other tiles, just larger than
them.

Not every theme needs one. If the images are more consistently strong with no single
standout, or the brief calls for an even, catalog-like feel, skip the hero role
entirely and let all tiles be wide/tall/normal/small — this is a legitimate choice,
not a fallback.

## 2. Shot-type variety, not just color variety

A board built entirely from wide establishing shots feels flat. A board built entirely
from macro texture shots feels claustrophobic. Aim to cover most of these per board:

- **Hero / establishing** — the clearest single image of the mood (1)
- **Environment / wide** — space, light, place (1-2)
- **Detail / object** — a specific thing that grounds the theme (2-3)
- **Texture / material** — surface, fabric, grain, pattern — these do a lot of the
  "feel" work and are easy to under-source (2-3)
- **Human / figure** (if relevant to the theme) — posture, gesture, expression (0-2)
- **Graphic / typographic accent** — optional, a texture or shape that reads more like
  a design element than a photo

Six to sixteen images is the usual working range. Fewer than six rarely covers enough
shot-type variety; more than sixteen usually means the board hasn't been curated hard
enough — cut the images that are almost-but-not-quite on theme. If the user explicitly
wants a denser, "maxed out" board, 16-18 is workable, but only if the extra images are
earning distinct jobs (more wide/establishing shots for mood, more texture/detail shots
for specificity) rather than padding out one category.

## 3. Color harmony — name it before you pick

Before searching, decide (or infer from the brief) which harmony the palette should use.
This makes curation much faster because you can reject off-palette images fast instead of
agonizing over each one:

- **Monochrome** — one hue, varying lightness/saturation. Calm, controlled, often
  luxury or minimalist briefs.
- **Analogous** — hues next to each other on the wheel (e.g. teal/blue/violet). Cohesive,
  natural, easy to make feel "designed."
- **Complementary** — opposite hues (e.g. rust/teal, mustard/indigo). High energy,
  good for bold or playful briefs — use a 70/30 split, not 50/50, or it fights itself.
- **Triadic** — three evenly-spaced hues. Vibrant, needs a neutral (black, white, stone)
  to anchor it or it reads chaotic.
- **Neutral + one accent** — mostly greige/stone/charcoal with a single accent color
  doing all the emotional work. Common for editorial, architectural, or "quiet luxury"
  briefs.

When curating, mentally sort candidate images into "on palette" and "off palette."
One or two deliberately off-palette images can add texture and prevent the board from
looking like a paint chip — more than that reads as sloppy curation.

## 4. Composition of the board itself

The board is itself a composition — treat it like one image made of many:

- **Weight balance, not symmetry.** Distribute visual heaviness (dark, saturated, busy
  images) across the board rather than clustering it in one corner. A board that's dark
  and dense on the left and pale and empty on the right feels unbalanced even though
  each individual tile is fine.
- **Breathing room.** Leave real gaps between tiles (consistent gutter, not cramped).
  A palette strip or a solid-color block can function as negative space and give the eye
  somewhere to rest.
- **Rhythm over grid uniformity.** Vary tile sizes in a pattern that feels intentional —
  large/small/medium repeating, not random and not perfectly uniform. A perfectly even
  grid of same-size tiles is the fastest way to make a board look like a stock-photo
  dump instead of a curated mood.
- **Anchor with a palette strip.** A small strip of 4-6 solid color swatches (pulled
  from the actual images, not guessed) does more to make a board look "finished" than
  almost any other single element. Put it somewhere it can act as a visual anchor —
  top edge, bottom edge, or as one grid cell among the images.

## 5. Typography, if the board has a title

Match letterforms to mood, the same way you'd match image content to mood:

- **Moody / romantic / vintage** → a serif with some personality (didone, slab, or
  humanist serif), often set at a larger size with generous letter-spacing
- **Modern / clean / tech** → a geometric or grotesque sans, tight tracking
- **Organic / handmade / warm** → a hand-lettered or brush script, used sparingly
  (never for body text, only for the title)
- **Editorial / fashion** → a high-contrast serif or a condensed all-caps sans

Keep it to one display font for the title plus one simple sans for any labels
(palette hex codes) — two fonts total, never more. Don't put source links or credit
labels on the image tiles themselves — keep the board visually clean.

## 6. Quick self-check before finalizing

Ask these before calling a board done:

1. Is there one obvious hero, or does every tile compete equally?
2. Could you name the color harmony in one phrase?
3. Is there at least one texture/material shot and one detail shot, not just wide scenes?
4. Is there a gap in visual weight (all-dark, all-light, all-busy) that needs balancing?
5. Does the palette strip actually match the colors in the images, or was it guessed?

If any of these fail, it's cheaper to swap one or two images than to rebuild the layout.
